Folkway, More, or Taboo?

Norms are the rules and expectations a group uses to guide its members’ behavior. Sociologists sort them by how strongly people react when someone breaks one. Sort these 16 behaviors as they’d be seen in the United States today. Some are easy. Others are meant to start an argument.

Folkway

An everyday custom. Breaking it gets you odd looks or mild annoyance.

More (“MOR-ay”)

A norm tied to right and wrong. Breaking it gets you judged as a bad person, and sometimes punished.

Taboo

A norm so strong that breaking it, or even thinking about it, brings disgust or horror.

What the hard ones show

The categories are a scale, not three boxes. William Graham Sumner introduced “folkways” and “mores” in 1906 to describe how strongly a group cares about a rule. Plenty of norms sit between them, and people in the same society disagree about where.

The same act can land in different places. Eating beef is ordinary for most Americans and forbidden for many Hindus. Leaving a tip is expected in the U.S. and can be awkward in Japan. A norm belongs to a group, not to the act itself.

Norms move. Living together before marriage went from scandal to ordinary in a few decades. Smoking indoors and driving after drinking went the other way. When a norm changes category, it usually means some group worked to change it.

Some norms are also laws, and some aren’t. Drunk driving is a more and a crime. Adultery is a more that’s rarely prosecuted. Jaywalking is a law most people treat as less than a folkway.

Taboos work through disgust. Psychologists find that people often can’t explain why a taboo act is wrong, even when they’re certain it is. Anthropologists point out that what a group finds disgusting tends to mark the boundaries it cares most about: family, the body, food, and the dead. The word itself comes from the Pacific. In Tongan, tapu (also written tabu) means sacred or forbidden: set apart from ordinary use, often for the gods or for chiefs. British explorer James Cook brought it into English after visiting Tonga in 1777, when his Tongan guests refused to eat at his table because the food was tapu. Related words are used across the Pacific, such as tapu in Māori and kapu in Hawaiian.

Talk about it

  1. Which cards did your group disagree on? What does the disagreement tell you about the people in the room (age, region, religion, family)?
  2. Pick one “changed over time” card. Who pushed for the change, and who resisted?
  3. Think of a folkway you’ve broken on purpose or by accident. How did people let you know? Those reactions are called sanctions.
  4. Add a card of your own that would be sorted differently by your grandparents, or by someone from another country.
  5. Is anything a taboo everywhere? What would it take to find out?

About this page

The “usual answer” for each card reflects how introductory sociology typically classifies it for the contemporary United States. It’s a starting point for discussion, not a measured fact, and several cards are deliberately borderline. Nothing you select is saved or sent anywhere.
